How I Balance Screen Time and Physical Health: My Personal Blueprint as a Remote Developer in Meru

By Eliphas Mutwiri Peter | Meru, Kenya

Introduction: The Moment I Realized My Digital Ambitions Needed Physical Boundaries

I remember working late into the night, somewhere around 2:00 AM, moving between a Termux session on my phone and my laptop while tracking code commits. I was updating my custom domain, managing static website deployments and working on digital publishing projects. My attention was completely absorbed by the screens.

The repositories were updating, the HTML files were changing and I was concentrating on getting everything right. But when I eventually stopped working and stood up from my chair, I became aware of how long I had remained in practically the same position. My lower back felt uncomfortable, my neck was stiff and my eyes were tired.

That experience made me reconsider something I had overlooked. While building digital assets and developing ideas about personal growth, I was giving much more attention to my projects than to the physical habits supporting my ability to work.

I had allowed long working sessions to become normal. Sometimes I would remain seated for hours, delay meals and continue checking my phone even after finishing computer work. I treated continuous screen immersion as evidence of commitment.

Eventually, I realised that professional progress should not require neglecting physical wellbeing. I did not want to abandon coding or stop building my website. I wanted to develop a working system that allowed me to pursue those ambitions while paying attention to movement, posture, sleep and recovery.

Working from Meru, Kenya, also taught me that healthier habits do not always require expensive equipment. Some improvements begin with changing the environment, setting boundaries and being more deliberate about everyday decisions.

1. How I Engineered Physical Friction to Break the Sedentary Loop

When I am deeply involved in debugging an HTML layout or writing long-form content, I find it easy to lose track of time. My attention becomes focused on solving the problem in front of me, and I often do not immediately notice that I have been sitting for a long period.

In the past, I relied heavily on reminders to tell me when to stand up. However, when I was concentrating, I would dismiss an alarm and continue working. I realised that I needed to make movement easier to incorporate into my environment rather than depending entirely on motivation.

Changing where I keep my water

One practical change I made was to stop keeping everything I needed within immediate reach. Instead of placing drinking water directly beside my laptop, I started keeping my water container on a separate table.

This meant I had to stand up and walk whenever I wanted a refill. It was a simple environmental adjustment, but it encouraged me to change position instead of remaining in the same place throughout a working session.

I also became more conscious of drinking water regularly. I do not treat a three-litre target as a requirement for everyone because individual fluid needs vary with activity, diet, weather and health.

The important lesson was not the size of the water container. It was discovering how small changes in the environment can encourage healthier behaviour.

Movement should not depend on finishing every task

I learned that waiting until all my work was complete before taking a break was unrealistic. There is almost always another website adjustment, article to write or technical issue to investigate.

I now consider short movement breaks part of my working process rather than interruptions to it.

2. The Layout Upgrades I Made to Support My Spine and Neck

For a long time, I worked directly from a standard laptop positioned flat on my desk. When using my smartphone to run Termux, I sometimes found myself bending my neck down for extended periods.

The problem was not that using a laptop or smartphone was inherently unhealthy. It was the way I positioned my body while using those devices and how long I remained in the same position.

Raising the laptop screen

I began looking for an affordable way to improve my workstation without investing in expensive office furniture.

One option I tried was using a stable stack of thick textbooks to elevate my laptop. The purpose was to bring the display closer to a comfortable viewing height and reduce the need to constantly look down.

However, raising the laptop also raises its keyboard. For extended working sessions, I found that an external keyboard and mouse are useful because they allow the screen and typing position to be adjusted independently.

Creating a more comfortable typing position

I pay attention to several practical details:

  • I ensure the screen is positioned directly in front of me.
  • I keep my shoulders relaxed.
  • I keep my elbows reasonably close to my body.
  • I make sure my wrists are not excessively bent while typing.
  • I provide comfortable support for my feet.
  • I change positions instead of forcing myself to maintain one posture all day.

These adjustments do not guarantee that discomfort will disappear, but I find they make my workstation more comfortable and reduce unnecessary strain.

The OSHA computer workstation guidance provides useful information about workstation positioning.

3. Protecting My Vision: How I Manage Digital Eye Strain

There have been working sessions when my eyes felt tired by the afternoon. Reading small text, inspecting HTML structures and concentrating on terminal output for extended periods made me more aware of the importance of visual breaks.

One thing I learned is that digital eye strain is not simply a blue-light problem. Extended close-up work can contribute to temporary discomfort, dryness, headaches and blurred vision.

Using the 20-20-20 approach

A practical technique I use is the 20-20-20 approach. After approximately every twenty minutes of close screen work, I look at an object around twenty feet away for at least twenty seconds.

This encourages me to interrupt continuous close-up focusing. I also make an effort to blink normally and adjust my screen position and lighting to reduce glare.

Adjusting screen brightness and appearance

I prefer comfortable display settings rather than extremely bright screens, especially when working in a dim environment.

I use dark themes because I find them more comfortable to use, and I also experiment with night-light settings during evening sessions.

However, I do not consider blue-light filters a guaranteed method of preventing eye damage. I know they should not replace regular breaks, suitable lighting or professional eye care when symptoms persist.

The American Academy of Ophthalmology's digital eye-care guidance provides additional information.

4. Separating Mind and Code: My Rules for Mental and Physical Disconnection

One of the hardest challenges I faced was establishing boundaries between work and personal time.

When you maintain an independent website hosted on GitHub Pages, the platform remains accessible even when you are not working. I often noticed there was always something that could be improved, an article that could be edited or an analytics report that could be checked.

In the past, I could finish editing a website and immediately open another digital platform. I might check analytics, review code commits or look at notifications shortly before going to sleep.

I realised that completing one screen-based activity and immediately starting another did not give me much opportunity to disconnect.

My 9:00 PM digital curfew

One boundary I decided to experiment with was a 9:00 PM stopping time. My intention was to finish important development tasks, review my changes and push completed work before closing my laptop.

A typical final command for my website updates is: 

git add .
git commit -m "Update website content"
git push origin main

After completing the necessary work, I aim to leave the remaining tasks for the following day rather than repeatedly reopening my laptop.

The 9:00 PM time is my personal working boundary, not a universal requirement. Different people have different responsibilities and working schedules.

Rediscovering the value of walking

I also appreciate taking walks around my neighbourhood in Meru. Instead of carrying my digital work into every free moment, I use walking as an opportunity to change my surroundings and give myself time away from screens.

I do not need a complicated fitness programme every time I want to move. I find that sometimes a simple walk is enough to break the pattern of sitting and looking at a display.

I have also found that stepping away from a difficult technical problem gives me an opportunity to approach it with a fresh perspective when I return.

5. Making Physical Activity Part of My Daily Routine

As a remote developer, I realized I do not need to spend every free hour in a gym to become more physically active. I found there are many practical activities that can fit into an ordinary day.

Walking, household activities, cycling and appropriate bodyweight exercises have all helped me contribute to my daily physical activity.

Simple activities I incorporate

  • I take short walks between demanding working sessions.
  • I stand up and change position regularly.
  • I use gentle stretching when appropriate.
  • I include suitable bodyweight exercises in my weekly routine.
  • I choose active household tasks instead of spending every break sitting.

I read that the World Health Organization recommends adults aim for at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity each week, alongside muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days.

I know that people who are currently inactive can begin gradually and increase activity according to their abilities.

I have also explored the importance of consistent training, nutrition and recovery in my guide: How to Build Healthy Muscle: A Beginner's Guide.

6. Improving My Digital Hygiene as a Remote Developer

I learned that physical health is only one part of working responsibly with technology. The way I manage my digital environments also influences how I spend my time and attention.

For example, unnecessary notifications can interrupt my concentration. I found that poor device organisation can make everyday tasks more frustrating, while unsafe digital practices can expose personal information.

I discovered that a healthy working environment should consider both physical and digital habits.

This includes organising my files, managing notifications, keeping software updated and being careful with unfamiliar links and online opportunities.

I discuss these habits in more detail in my article: Essential Digital Hygiene for Remote Developers.

I also believe that understanding how personal information is collected and used is an important part of responsible technology use.

For a deeper discussion, read: How I Audit and Protect My Digital Footprint.

7. Protecting Sleep and Allowing My Body to Recover

Late-night development can be tempting, especially when a project is close to completion. I completely understand the feeling of wanting to correct one more issue before closing the laptop.

However, I noticed that allowing digital work to continue indefinitely can interfere with the time available for sleep.

I know sleep is important for concentration, memory, recovery and general wellbeing. I understand that most adults need at least seven hours of sleep each night.

I consider it useful to establish a reasonable stopping time, reduce unnecessary evening screen activity and create a calmer routine before going to bed.

I also try to avoid treating every notification as something that requires an immediate response.

I believe a successful working routine should leave room for recovery, not just productivity.

8. My Practical Screen-Time and Physical Health Routine

I do not believe that a routine needs to be complicated to be useful. I find what matters is making healthy decisions consistently rather than depending on occasional motivation.

Morning

  • I begin with personal care and a balanced breakfast.
  • I drink water according to my needs.
  • I make time for walking or other physical activity.
  • I prepare a comfortable working environment.

During working hours

  • I break up prolonged sitting with regular movement.
  • I look away from the screen periodically.
  • I maintain a comfortable workstation position.
  • I eat meals instead of repeatedly postponing them.
  • I manage unnecessary notifications.

Evening

  • I finish important tasks and close unnecessary applications.
  • I spend time away from digital devices.
  • I include relaxing activities that do not require a screen.
  • I prepare for a consistent sleeping schedule.

I share this as a flexible example rather than a strict medical programme. I believe everyone can adapt it to their working hours, responsibilities and individual circumstances.

9. Staying Focused Without Sacrificing Your Health

One important lesson from managing my website is that focus should not be confused with continuous screen exposure.

I know there are times when working without interruption is useful. However, I learned that concentration does not mean ignoring physical needs or remaining in one position for hours.

I have become more interested in creating working sessions that allow me to concentrate on a specific task, take appropriate breaks and return with a clearer mind.

This also means accepting that not every technical problem needs to be solved immediately. I found some decisions become easier after stepping away and looking at the issue again.

I explore this relationship between attention, productivity and independent development in: How to Stay Focused While Coding.

Conclusion: Your Body Is Part of Your Professional Life

There is one important lesson I have taken from building digital projects: professional progress should not be measured only by what appears on a computer screen.

A website can become faster, an article can become more informative and a development project can become more advanced. But I believe none of these achievements should make us forget that physical wellbeing matters.

I did not develop better working habits overnight. I started by thinking about where I kept my water, how I positioned my laptop and how often I moved away from my screen. I also began paying more attention to my evening routine and the time I spent checking digital platforms.

I share these as practical changes, not a perfect formula. Some days are more organised than others for me, and I know there is always room to improve.

My biggest takeaway is that technology should support our lives rather than consume every part of them.

I encourage you to continue building your projects, learning new skills and pursuing your ambitions. Just remember that your physical health deserves attention along the way.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. How can I balance screen time and physical health?

Break up long periods of sitting, take regular movement breaks, maintain a comfortable workstation, rest your eyes and establish boundaries between work and recreational screen use.

2. What is the 20-20-20 rule?

After approximately every twenty minutes of close screen work, look at something around twenty feet away for at least twenty seconds. It is a practical way to interrupt prolonged close-up focusing.

3. Does blue light from computer screens permanently damage the eyes?

Ordinary blue light from computer screens has not been shown to cause eye disease. However, prolonged screen use can contribute to temporary eye discomfort, dryness and visual fatigue.

4. How often should remote workers take breaks?

There is no single break schedule that works for everyone. Regularly changing position, standing and walking for short periods can help reduce prolonged sedentary behaviour.

5. Can I improve my workstation without buying expensive furniture?

Yes. A stable laptop stand, external keyboard, mouse and appropriate chair adjustments can help create a more comfortable working environment.

6. How much physical activity should adults aim for?

The WHO recommends 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity each week for adults, alongside muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days.

7. Why is sleep important for remote developers?

Adequate sleep supports concentration, memory, recovery and general health. Most adults need at least seven hours of sleep each night.
