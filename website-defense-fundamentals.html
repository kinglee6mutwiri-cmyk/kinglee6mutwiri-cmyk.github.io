Website Defense Fundamentals: How I Protect My Own Site From Attackers

By Eliphas Mutwiri Peter • November 2026 • Practical Website Security Guide

When I first started publishing my writing online, I used to think website security was something only big companies with massive servers had to worry about. My thinking was simple: why would anyone bother attacking a personal publication?

That mindset changed the moment I looked closely at how the modern internet actually works. Attackers rarely sit at a keyboard picking individual victims one by one. Automated scripts and scanning tools can examine large numbers of domains looking for easy mistakes: an unlocked domain registrar, a leaked API key inside a public repository, an expired SSL certificate, or an outdated plugin nobody bothered to update.

Over time, as I built, maintained and improved my own site, I developed a straightforward defensive routine. I do not rely on complicated enterprise tools. I focus on closing the obvious doors first. I believe a website owner should understand the basic systems controlling a website before investing in complicated security products.

This is particularly important for independent publishers, developers and people building their first online projects. A website may look like a collection of pages and images to a visitor, but behind that simple appearance are accounts, domain settings, files, deployment processes and external services that need protection.

Here is the practical approach I use to think about website protection, together with steps other website owners can apply to their own projects.

1. I Treat My Domain Registrar Like a Bank Vault

If someone takes control of your hosting server, you may be able to rebuild the website from a backup. If someone hijacks your domain registrar account, however, the situation can be much more serious. Your domain is the address people use to reach your website. Whoever controls its registration and DNS settings may be able to redirect visitors somewhere else.

Imagine spending years building an online publication, developing its reputation and encouraging readers to return. A person who gains unauthorised control of the domain could redirect that traffic to a fraudulent page. Readers might believe the fake page belongs to you because it uses an address they already recognise.

That is why I consider the domain registrar one of the most important accounts connected to a website. I would not treat it like an ordinary account used for entertainment or casual browsing. It controls something essential to the identity and availability of the publication.

Use strong multi-factor authentication

A password alone is not always enough to protect an important account. Passwords can be guessed, reused from another compromised service, stolen through phishing or exposed through insecure devices.

Multi-factor authentication adds another verification step. Where supported, I prefer an authenticator application or a hardware security key over relying only on SMS messages. Text-message verification can be vulnerable to certain attacks, including SIM swapping.

The important lesson is to protect both the password and the recovery process. An account with a strong password can still become vulnerable if its recovery email is poorly protected.

Keep domain transfer protection enabled

Domain registrars commonly provide transfer-lock features that help prevent unauthorised transfers to another registrar. I consider this an important protective setting because an attacker should not be able to move control of a domain simply by gaining access to a weak account.

I would also recommend reviewing the contact details associated with the domain. Incorrect recovery information can create difficulties when an owner needs to verify ownership or respond to a legitimate registrar request.

Protect the recovery email account

The email account connected to your registrar deserves the same attention as the registrar itself. If someone compromises that mailbox, they may receive password-reset messages and other sensitive account notifications.

I therefore recommend using a unique password, enabling multi-factor authentication, reviewing recovery methods and checking active account sessions. Remove devices and application permissions that you no longer recognise.

This is one reason I believe a website security review should not stop at the website itself. It should include the accounts that control it. My Annual Digital Security Audit provides another useful way to think about reviewing important digital accounts.

2. I Keep My Architecture Simple and Attack Surface Small

One of the best security decisions I made for my website was resisting the urge to install heavy software I did not actually need. Every database, third-party plugin, contact form script and administrative interface introduces another component that may need maintenance and protection.

Security professionals use the term attack surface to describe the possible points through which an attacker might interact with a system. A larger attack surface can mean more opportunities for configuration errors, vulnerable components and unintended exposure.

This does not mean every additional feature is dangerous. Websites need functionality to serve their readers. The important distinction is between a necessary feature and one that adds complexity without providing enough value to justify its maintenance requirements.

Why I value lightweight websites

My website follows a lightweight, static-first approach. Its main content is delivered through HTML pages, stylesheets and image assets. A traditional database is not required simply to display those static pages.

This reduces the number of server-side components involved in delivering the content. It also makes the website easier to inspect, maintain and back up.

However, static does not mean invulnerable. A static website can still be affected by a compromised domain account, stolen repository credentials, malicious changes to its source files, unsafe third-party scripts or incorrect hosting configuration.

I would never describe a simple website as automatically secure. Simplicity reduces certain risks, but the owner must still protect the systems used to publish and maintain it.

Remove unnecessary software

If you use a content management system such as WordPress, regularly review your installed plugins and themes. Software that is no longer needed should be removed rather than forgotten.

Deactivating a plugin is not always equivalent to removing its files. Depending on the software, unused components may still remain on the server. They may also become outdated and create unnecessary maintenance obligations.

Before removing anything, check whether your website depends on it. Keep a backup, understand what the component does and avoid making changes blindly.

Be careful with external services

Analytics, advertising, embedded content and other external services can add useful functionality. They can also introduce dependencies outside your direct control.

Before adding an external script, I recommend checking its source, confirming that it belongs to the intended provider and understanding what it does. Avoid copying JavaScript from unknown websites simply because someone claims it will improve your traffic or revenue.

A useful website is not necessarily the one with the largest number of features. It is the one whose features are necessary, maintained and appropriate for its purpose.

3. I Guard My Git Repository and Terminal Workflow

I manage and push updates to my website directly from the command line using Git. This workflow gives me visibility over changes to my website files, but it also comes with one golden rule: never commit secrets to a public repository.

A public repository can expose source code to anyone who visits it. That is useful for transparency and collaboration, but it becomes a serious problem when private information is accidentally included.

A lesson every builder should remember: Once you push an API key, password or private token to a public repository, deleting it from the latest version does not automatically remove it from the commit history. It may already have been copied by automated scanners.

Understand what should remain private

Some information is intended to be public. HTML pages, public images, stylesheets and ordinary website content normally belong in a public website repository.

Other information must remain private. This includes account passwords, private API keys, authentication tokens, private SSH keys, database credentials and sensitive configuration values.

I recommend separating public website content from private credentials. If a project needs secrets during development or deployment, use an appropriate secret management mechanism rather than writing them directly into public source files.

Review files before publishing

One habit that makes a difference is checking the working directory before committing changes. A directory may contain temporary scripts, old copies, test files or personal notes that were never intended for public distribution.

Git provides simple commands that help a developer understand what is about to be published.

  • git status displays changed and untracked files.
  • git diff shows unstaged changes.
  • git diff --cached displays staged changes.
  • git log --oneline -5 reviews recent commits.

I consider these commands useful because they encourage deliberate publishing instead of treating every file in a directory as something that should automatically become public.

Use SSH keys responsibly

SSH keys provide a cryptographic method of authenticating Git operations when the hosting service supports them. They can reduce dependence on repeatedly entering account passwords during repository operations.

A private SSH key must remain private. Do not upload it to a public repository, send it to another person or leave it unprotected on a shared device.

If you believe a private key has been exposed, remove its authorisation from the relevant service and create a replacement. Simply deleting the file from your current directory is not enough if an attacker may already have copied it.

Be cautious when using Termux

A mobile terminal can be a practical environment for maintaining a lightweight website. However, using a phone does not remove the normal responsibilities of software security.

Keep your device protected with an appropriate screen lock. Install tools from trusted sources, review commands before executing them and avoid copying unfamiliar scripts that request broad permissions.

When working with Git, check which account is authenticated and which repository you are modifying. This is particularly important when switching between personal projects or working directories.

My broader lesson is that a good publishing workflow should make mistakes easier to notice. A few seconds spent reviewing changes can prevent an unnecessary security problem.

4. I Enforce HTTPS Everywhere Without Exception

There is no good reason to leave a modern public website unnecessarily exposed to unencrypted HTTP connections. HTTPS uses Transport Layer Security, commonly called TLS, to protect information travelling between a visitor's browser and the website server.

Without HTTPS, information sent through an unencrypted connection may be exposed to interception or modification by someone capable of monitoring that network connection. This is especially concerning when a website handles account details, forms or other private information.

On my site, HTTPS is the primary version of the website. HTTP requests should redirect to their corresponding HTTPS addresses. This helps visitors reach the intended secure version rather than encountering different versions of the same page.

Understand what HTTPS actually protects

HTTPS provides several important protections. It helps encrypt information in transit, makes it harder for third parties to modify that information without detection and allows the browser to authenticate the server through its digital certificate.

These protections are valuable, but HTTPS is not a complete security solution. It cannot prevent someone from stealing a website owner's password through phishing. It cannot automatically remove malicious JavaScript from a website. It also cannot guarantee that a website operator handles personal information responsibly.

A fraudulent website can also use HTTPS. The secure connection only tells the browser that the connection to that domain is protected; it does not prove that the organisation behind the domain is honest.

Keep certificates valid

A website's TLS certificate needs to be valid and correctly configured. An expired certificate or a certificate that does not match the requested domain can trigger browser security warnings.

Website owners should understand how their hosting provider issues and renews certificates. If renewal is automated, it is still sensible to monitor the process rather than assume that it will never fail.

Use consistent canonical URLs

Canonical URLs help search engines understand which address represents the preferred version of a page. A page should identify its own correct canonical address rather than sending every article to the homepage.

For example, this article should use its complete HTTPS address as its canonical URL. Other pages should each have their own appropriate canonical address.

Consistency also matters for internal links. Using the preferred HTTPS version helps reduce confusion between HTTP and HTTPS addresses and between alternative domain versions.

My practical rule: HTTPS is an essential part of website protection, but it must work alongside secure accounts, careful publishing practices and responsible maintenance.

5. I Defend Against Phishing Aimed at Site Owners

Technical firewalls cannot stop you from handing over your own account details if you fall for a convincing fraudulent message. This is why I believe human awareness is an important part of website security.

Once a website becomes visible online, its owner may receive emails claiming that the domain is about to expire, an SSL certificate has failed, a copyright complaint has been filed or an advertising account needs immediate verification.

Some messages are legitimate. Others are designed to create panic and pressure the recipient into visiting a fraudulent website.

Recognise the pressure tactics

Phishing messages often try to make people act before thinking. They may claim that an account will be suspended within minutes, that a payment has failed or that important content will be removed unless the recipient signs in immediately.

The urgency itself is not proof of fraud, but it should encourage you to slow down and verify the message independently.

Look at the sender's actual email address rather than relying only on the display name. Be cautious about unexpected attachments, unusual requests for payment and links that lead to unfamiliar domains.

Visit official dashboards directly

Whenever an unexpected urgent message lands in my inbox, I do not automatically click the login link inside it. Instead, I open a fresh browser tab, type the official registrar or hosting address myself and check the account dashboard.

If a genuine problem exists, it should normally be possible to confirm it through the official account or support channel. This independent verification reduces the risk of entering credentials into a fake login page.

Never disclose authentication codes

Verification codes are designed to prove that the account holder controls an authorised device or authentication method. Giving a code to someone who contacts you unexpectedly can help that person complete an account takeover.

Do not share one-time codes, recovery codes or private authentication information with people claiming to be support agents.

If you suspect that you have entered your password into a fraudulent page, act quickly. Visit the genuine service directly, change the affected password, revoke suspicious sessions and review recovery information and connected applications.

Think beyond email

Phishing attempts can also arrive through text messages, social media, messaging applications and fake technical support conversations. A website owner should be cautious whenever an unexpected communication asks for account access, payment or confidential information.

I believe the safest habit is simple: verify independently before taking action. A message that looks professional is not necessarily genuine.

6. I Maintain Clean Local Backups

Even with strong defenses, accidents happen. You might accidentally overwrite an important file during an edit, make an incorrect change or experience an interruption from an upstream service.

Because I maintain a local copy of my website directory alongside my remote Git repository, I have another way to recover my work if something goes wrong.

Backups are particularly important for independent website owners because a small mistake can affect a large part of the publication. A missing image, damaged stylesheet or overwritten HTML page can reduce the quality of the visitor experience.

Do not rely on only one copy

Keeping a website directory on one phone or computer is useful, but it should not be the only recovery option. If the device is lost, damaged or compromised, the local copy may disappear too.

A stronger strategy keeps independent copies in separate locations. For example, a website owner may keep a working copy, a remote repository and another secure backup that is not dependent on the same device.

The important point is independence. If every copy is stored in the same account or on the same device, one incident could affect all of them.

Back up the files needed to rebuild the website

A useful website backup should include more than the main HTML pages. Images, stylesheets, scripts and other assets may be essential to restoring the original appearance and functionality.

It is also useful to preserve documentation describing the domain configuration, publishing process and important project structure. That information can make recovery easier when the owner has to rebuild the website on a new device.

Private credentials must be handled separately. A backup should not become a new place where passwords, tokens or private keys are exposed.

Test whether restoration works

A backup is only useful if the files can be recovered. I recommend testing restoration periodically instead of assuming that a successful copy operation guarantees a working recovery process.

A simple test might involve restoring a copy of the website into a separate directory and checking whether the pages and images are present. For larger websites, the test should also consider databases, configuration and any required dependencies.

Keep a recovery plan

Website owners should know where their backups are stored, when the most recent copy was made and how to restore it. It is much easier to make these decisions before an emergency than after a website has already stopped working.

I see backups as a form of preparation rather than an admission that security has failed. Responsible website maintenance includes planning for mistakes, service interruptions and unexpected events.

7. I Review Website Files and Configuration Regularly

A website can become untidy over time. New pages are added, old files remain in the directory, temporary scripts are created and configurations change as the project develops.

This is why I believe website maintenance should include periodic reviews. The purpose is not simply to make the directory look organised. It is to understand what is published and whether any unnecessary exposure has been introduced.

Check for forgotten files

Backup copies, old test pages and temporary files should not automatically be placed in a public website directory. Some files may contain information that was never intended for visitors.

Review the directory and identify files that are genuinely needed. Before deleting anything, make sure it is not used by another page or part of the publishing process.

Check access permissions

Access permissions determine which users and processes can read, modify or execute files. Website owners should avoid granting broader permissions than a component actually needs.

If a hosting provider manages the server configuration, follow its documented recommendations rather than applying random permission changes from online tutorials.

Review third-party dependencies

Libraries, plugins and external services can become outdated. An older component may contain a vulnerability that has already been corrected in a newer version.

Review the components your website actually uses, check whether updates are available and obtain updates from trusted sources. Do not install an unfamiliar package simply because it promises a quick fix.

8. I Take Website Privacy and Visitor Trust Seriously

Website security is closely connected to privacy. Visitors may share information through forms, email subscriptions, account registrations or other website features. Website owners should understand what information they collect and why they need it.

Collecting information without a clear purpose creates unnecessary responsibility. A website should not request sensitive information simply because a form makes it technically possible.

Collect only necessary information

Data minimisation means limiting collection to information that is relevant and necessary for a defined purpose. For a simple publication, there may be little reason to collect extensive personal details from ordinary readers.

If a website uses analytics, advertising or other external services, its owner should understand the relevant data practices and provide appropriate privacy information to visitors.

Keep privacy information understandable

A privacy policy should explain what information is collected, how it is used, whether third-party services are involved and how visitors can contact the website owner about privacy questions.

The policy should describe the actual website practices rather than copying statements that may not apply. Accuracy matters more than using complicated legal language.

For another perspective on protecting personal information and understanding online exposure, read my guide to data privacy and digital footprints .

Protect visitor trust

Trust takes time to build and can be damaged quickly. A website that uses misleading forms, deceptive login pages or unsafe external scripts can put visitors at risk.

Website owners should be transparent about the services they use and avoid unnecessary collection or disclosure of visitor information.

9. I Understand That Website Security Is an Ongoing Process

One mistake website owners can make is treating security as a task completed once during the initial launch. A website changes over time. New pages are published, software is updated, accounts are added and external services change their requirements.

A setup that was appropriate several months ago may need attention today. Security therefore requires regular observation and maintenance.

Review important accounts

Periodically check the accounts connected to your website, including the registrar, hosting provider, repository and email service. Review authorised users, connected applications and active sessions.

Pay attention to unusual changes

Unexpected changes to website files, unfamiliar repository activity, unusual account notifications or changes to DNS settings deserve investigation.

Not every unusual event indicates an attack. However, checking the source of an unexpected change is better than ignoring it.

Know how to respond to a problem

If you suspect an account has been compromised, use a trusted device to access the official service. Change affected credentials, revoke suspicious sessions and review recovery methods.

If a secret has been exposed in a public repository, revoke or rotate it at the service that issued it. Removing the visible text alone does not guarantee that the secret is no longer usable.

If a website has been modified without authorisation, preserve relevant information where possible, investigate the affected accounts and restore trusted files from a clean backup after addressing the cause.

My Personal 6-Step Website Defense Checklist

These are the six practical areas I would review first when securing a personal website or independent online publication.

Website Security Checklist

  • Lock the domain: Enable multi-factor authentication, protect recovery methods and enable registrar transfer protection.
  • Enforce HTTPS: Redirect HTTP traffic to HTTPS, verify certificate validity and monitor certificate renewal.
  • Audit website files: Remove unnecessary files, abandoned software and unused dependencies after checking their purpose.
  • Protect credentials: Keep passwords, private keys, tokens and sensitive configuration values out of public source code.
  • Verify urgent emails: Visit official account dashboards directly instead of following unexpected login links.
  • Keep independent backups: Maintain updated copies in a separate secure location and test restoration periodically.

Final Thoughts: Security Starts With Everyday Decisions

You do not need to be a full-time security engineer to take meaningful steps towards protecting a website. Most small website owners can improve their security by understanding the accounts and systems that control their online presence.

My experience building and maintaining a website through a terminal-based workflow has taught me that security is not one action completed during launch. It is a series of decisions repeated throughout the life of a website.

A secure domain account, a clean repository, HTTPS, protected credentials and reliable backups are not separate ideas with no relationship. Together, they form a practical foundation for reducing avoidable risks.

Start with the systems that control your domain and website. Understand where your files are stored, review what you publish, protect the accounts that can change your website and establish a recovery process before you need one.

No single measure can guarantee that a website will never experience a security incident. However, reducing unnecessary exposure and responding carefully to problems can make a website more resilient.

When I think about website defense, I return to one simple principle: do not wait for an incident to discover which parts of your website you should have protected.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What are website defense fundamentals?

Website defense fundamentals are the basic security practices used to protect a website, its accounts, files, visitors and infrastructure. They include domain protection, HTTPS, strong authentication, secure credentials, software maintenance and reliable backups.

2. Can a small personal website be attacked?

Yes. Automated scanning tools can discover websites and search for common weaknesses without an attacker specifically selecting the website owner. Small websites should therefore follow appropriate security practices too.

3. Does HTTPS make a website completely secure?

No. HTTPS protects information travelling between a browser and a website server. It does not prevent phishing, compromised accounts, malicious scripts or insecure website configurations.

4. Why should I protect my Git repository?

A public repository can expose private information if passwords, API keys or authentication tokens are committed accidentally. Reviewing changes and keeping secrets outside public source code reduces this risk.

5. How often should I back up my website?

Backups should be made regularly and whenever important changes are introduced. The appropriate frequency depends on how often the website changes and how much information the owner can afford to lose. Restoration should also be tested periodically.

6. What is the first step in protecting a website?

Start by securing the accounts that control your domain and website. Use strong unique passwords, enable multi-factor authentication, protect recovery methods and review who has administrative access.

7. Can a static website still have security problems?

Yes. Static websites reduce some server-side risks, but they can still be affected by compromised domain accounts, stolen repository credentials, malicious source changes, unsafe external scripts and incorrect hosting configurations.

8. What should I do if I accidentally publish a secret?

Revoke or rotate the exposed credential through the service that issued it. Remove it from the repository and review the commit history. Deleting the visible copy alone does not guarantee that the credential is no longer usable.

9. Why is a domain registrar account important?

A registrar account controls important domain registration settings. If an attacker gains access, they may be able to make unauthorised changes or attempt to transfer the domain. Strong authentication and transfer protection help reduce this risk.

10. Are website backups enough to guarantee recovery?

No. Backups are important, but recovery also depends on their integrity, accessibility and completeness. Website owners should keep independent copies and test the restoration process.
